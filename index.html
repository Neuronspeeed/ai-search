How search works

How a search engine finds pages and puts them in order: inverted indexes and BM25, click signals, knowledge graphs, dense vectors, hybrid fusion, NDCG, and RAG.

  • Three dimensions of search intent. Good relevance combines Content understanding (matching keywords, language patterns, and attributes), User understanding (each searcher's preferences and history), and Domain understanding (what words, phrases, and entities mean in your own domain).
  • Reflected intelligence. Every query, click, and conversion feeds back into the search engine. Instead of hand-writing rules forever, the system learns domain language and ranking weights from real traffic. It comes in four families: popularized (signals boosting), personalized (collaborative filtering), generalized (learning to rank), and knowledge graph learning (related terms and misspellings from query logs). See the table in §3.
  • Four architectural layers. Sparse lexical retrieval (BM25), domain query understanding (knowledge graphs), learned ranking (signals, personalization, LTR), and neural retrieval (dense vectors, hybrid fusion, RAG).

§0 · Primer (Skip if you already know this)

From raw strings to postings and vectors

A search engine does not scan your docs word by word at query time. At index time, an analyzer typically lowercases each document, strips punctuation, and may drop stop words and stem terms. The same analyzer runs on the query, so both sides produce the same terms. The engine then builds an inverted index where each term points straight to the list of documents containing it.

Eight ideas the rest of the page is built on

§0.a · Inverted index
A lookup table from words to pages. AND and OR are just set math.

The engine never reads pages at search time. It looks each word up and gets back the list of pages that contain it. AND keeps pages in both lists. OR keeps pages in either.

§0.b · Diminishing returns
The 10th mention shouldn't count like the 1st. A curve that bends and flattens.

Raw counting gives every repeat the same value, so spam wins. A saturating curve gives the first few mentions most of the credit and later ones almost none. BM25 (§2) is built on this.

§0.c · Rarity is information
Rare words tell you more. Common words tell you almost nothing.

If a word is on almost every page, finding it on one more page says nothing. If it's on 3 pages out of 1,000, it's a strong clue. That's IDF: weight = log(pages ÷ pages with the word).

§0.d · Precision vs. recall
Show more, find more, but get messier. You can't max both.

Precision: of what you showed, how much was right. Recall: of everything right, how much you showed. Moving the cutoff down a ranked list trades one for the other.

§0.e · Position matters
A great result at #8 is worth less than at #1. Ranking metrics discount by position.

People (and metrics like NDCG, §11) care most about the top. A common discount is 1 ÷ log₂(rank + 1): rank 1 counts fully, rank 2 counts 63%, and it keeps shrinking.

2 clicks out of 3 views isn't a 67% page. Start from a sensible guess.

§0.g · Meaning as a place
Similar meanings sit close together. Search = find the nearest points.

An embedding turns text into a point. Phrases that mean the same thing land near each other, even with no shared words. Vector search (§7) returns the points nearest to your query.

  • Stemming vs. lemmatization. Stemming chops suffixes with simple rules (retries becomes retry, running becomes run; exact output depends on the stemmer). Lemmatization uses a dictionary so better maps to good, at higher CPU cost. Stemming adds false positives. Skip it when you need precision, such as normalizing queries for click signals or matching exact entities. For documents, a common fix is two fields, one stemmed and one unstemmed, with the unstemmed one boosted higher.
  • Stop-word pitfalls. Dropping common words like the or in shrinks the index, but breaks queries like "to be or not to be" or "Vitamin A". Modern BM25 engines often keep stop words and let low IDF down-weight them. Low IDF fixes ranking, not matching. Sparse vectors: direction vs. length

Cosine similarity looks at the direction of two vectors and ignores their length. The dot product multiplies coordinates out so length counts too.

  • Sparse vectors. TF-IDF and BM25 give every word its own slot, so almost every slot is zero. In a small apple juice example, the vocabulary has 48 slots and the query fills only 2 of them; engines store only the non-zero entries.
  • Direction. Cosine asks whether a document uses the query's words in the same proportions.
  • Length. The dot product also grows when a document repeats those words more often.

§1 · Eligibility vs. Ordering

Matching vs. ranking

Every search request runs in two steps. Matching decides which pages are eligible at all, which sets result counts and facets. It also keeps the work small, since scoring a few hundred matches is far cheaper than scoring every page. Filters and the query do different jobs. A filter like category = docs only removes pages; the query removes pages and scores the survivors.

Production note: why filters are cached bitsets in Lucene / Elasticsearch

In Elasticsearch and OpenSearch, putting category = docs inside a filter clause (instead of a must clause) skips scoring completely. Once a filter is used often, Lucene caches its matching document IDs as a compact bitset, and later queries reuse it instead of re-running the filter.

If you accidentally put a filter in a scoring clause, you waste CPU scoring every document on category:docs, lose filter caching, and pollute your relevance scores with metadata noise.

§2 · Scoring Text

From word counts to BM25

TF-IDF multiplies how often a term appears in a document (TF) by how rare that term is across the corpus (IDF). Naive TF-IDF (raw counts, no length handling) is mostly a teaching exercise now, and two failure modes explain why.

BM25 under the hood: saturation and length

Someone is going through your docs for ECONNRESET. One page has it 5 times and is twice the length of the average page. Each part of BM25 has its own job on those 5 mentions:

  • TF (term frequency). The page has ECONNRESET 5 times. Raw TF-IDF gives that a value of 5.
  • IDF (rarity). An error code on a few pages is worth more than a common word like connection. It multiplies every curve by the same constant.
  • k₁ (saturation). Caps what repeats are worth. At k₁ = 1.2, the weight can never cross k₁ + 1 = 2.2.
  • b (length penalty). A long page has more chances to repeat any word. At b = 0.75, a 2× longer page scores 13% less than an average page with the same 5 mentions.
BM25 Scoring Formula (Textbook Form)
$$\text{BM25}(q, d) = \sum_{t \in q} \underbrace{\text{IDF}(t)}_{\text{rarity}} \cdot \frac{\text{tf}(t,d)\cdot(k_1 + 1)}{\text{tf}(t,d) + k_1 \cdot \left(1 - b + b \cdot \frac{|d|}{\text{avgdl}}\right)}$$
k₁ = TF saturation speed (ceiling is k₁ + 1) b = length penalty (0 = ignore length, 1 = full penalty) IDF(t) = ln(1 + (N − df + 0.5) / (df + 0.5)), the rarity curve above

  • Default to BM25 for lexical search. It is the baseline in Elasticsearch, OpenSearch, Lucene, and most RAG stacks.
  • Use it alongside embeddings, not instead of them. BM25 wins on exact matches (IDs, error codes, names, specialist jargon). Dense retrievers win on paraphrase and meaning. The common setup today is both together (hybrid search, §8), followed by a reranker.
  • Start with k₁ ≈ 1.2 and b ≈ 0.75. Tuning matters most when documents are very short or very uneven in length. Don't guess: grid-search k₁ and b on a small labeled set and keep the pair with the best NDCG@10 (the headline metric public benchmarks like BEIR report, §11).
Production gotcha: why Elasticsearch & Lucene scores look smaller than the textbook

Since version 8, Lucene (and therefore Elasticsearch, OpenSearch, and Solr) drops the constant (k₁ + 1) factor from the numerator because it is identical for every document matching a query. Dropping it never changes the ranking order, but it caps the TF component at 1.0 instead of k₁ + 1.

Expect different absolute scores in other tools. Python's rank_bm25, for example, keeps the (k₁ + 1) factor, defaults to k₁ = 1.5, and uses a slightly different IDF. Lucene also stores page length approximately, and Elasticsearch computes statistics per shard. Compare rankings, not raw scores. This page's explain trees show the textbook form, with TF parts up to k₁ + 1.

Field boosts, phrase boosts, and reading the score

Real pages have more than one field. A match in the title usually means more than a match deep in the body, so engines score each field with its own BM25 and multiply it by a field boost, such as qf = title^3 body^1. Phrase boosts (pf, pf2, pf3) add points when query words sit next to each other, without throwing out pages where they don't. When a ranking looks wrong, the engine's explain output shows every number that went into a score.

  • Field boosts (qf). title^10 description^2.5 makes a title match worth 10× and a description match worth 2.5×. Each field keeps its own term counts and its own average length. By default Solr takes the best-scoring field for each word (dismax; a tie setting blends in the others). The demo below simply adds the fields, like Elasticsearch's most_fields.
  • Phrase boosts (pf, pf2, pf3). pf boosts the full phrase, pf2 every two-word pair, and pf3 every three-word run. They only add score. They never remove a page.
  • The explain tree. For each matched word, the engine prints the boost, the IDF (with n and N), and the TF part (with freq, k1, b, dl, avgdl), then adds them up. Reading it is the first step in any relevance bug hunt.

Signals boosting: adding vs. multiplying, and time decay

        

          Click logs, saves, and resolved tickets tell you which pages help people. Two
          choices control how popularity interacts with BM25: whether you combine them by addition (+) or
          multiplication (×), and how quickly old clicks expire under half-life decay.
        


        

          Signals boosting is only the first of four families of reflected intelligence built from the same click logs:
          three ranking models, plus knowledge graph learning. Each learns a different kind of relevance, and each fails in a
          different place. That is why real stacks combine them instead of picking one.

§4 · Query Understanding

        
Query interpretation and semantic knowledge graphs

        

          When someone searches top redis cache guide or postgres driver timeout, treating every
          word as a plain keyword throws away structure. A semantic query pipeline runs
          four stages: parse entities and trigger words with an FST tagger, enrich them via a Semantic Knowledge Graph
          (SKG) built from your inverted index, transform triggers into filters and boosts, and search.
        


        
    
          
  • Query log mining. Users teach you your domain vocabulary when they reformulate failed queries. Mining query pairs surfaces domain synonyms (socket hang up and ECONNRESET), misspellings, and multi-word phrases automatically.
    • 
          
  • Two-level sense disambiguation. An ambiguous word like driver cannot be expanded blindly. A two-level SKG traversal first identifies the category (databases vs. golf or rideshare) and then expands terms only inside that subgraph.
    • 
          
  • Semantic functions. Trigger words like top, near, or under $50 do not belong in the keyword query. The transform step converts them into rating boosts, geospatial geofilt clauses, or range filters.
    • 
        

        

          Go deeper · 2 more notes
          
    
          
  • Explicit triples and Hearst patterns. Alongside statistical SKGs, Open Information Extraction parses sentences like "databases such as Postgres" into explicit RDF triples (Subject, Predicate, Object) stored in a sidecar collection for exact taxonomical hierarchies.
    • 
          
  • Knowledge modeling spectrum. Separate alternative labels (deterministic replacements for acronyms and typos), synonyms (broader recall expansion), and ontologies/taxonomies (structural is_a class hierarchies) instead of dumping everything into one synonym file.
    •

Where SKG relatedness scores come from

        

          Each [-1, +1] score above comes from a statistical test on document sets.
          Take every document that matches the foreground query (say pain). Count how often a
          candidate word (say advil) shows up there, and compare that with how often it shows up in the
          background (the whole index). If it appears far more often than chance, the two are related.
        


        

          
SKG Relatedness (z-score, then squashed to [−1, 1])

          
$$z(x) = \frac{|D_x \cap D_{fg}| \;-\; |D_{fg}| \cdot P_x}{\sqrt{|D_{fg}| \cdot P_x \,(1 - P_x)}} \qquad\qquad P_x = \frac{|D_x \cap D_{bg}|}{|D_{bg}|}$$

          

            D_fg = docs matching the foreground query (e.g. pain)
            P_x = chance a random background doc contains x
            z → [−1, 1] = a sigmoid squashes the score; 0 means "no link"
          

        


        
    
          
  • Reading the score. Near +1: strongly related (advil for pain). Near 0: no link, which is where stop words like the land, because they appear everywhere. Near −1: the word belongs to a different domain and avoids these docs.
    • 
          
  • Noise floor (min_occurrences). A word seen in only one or two matching docs can score high by luck. So you set a minimum number of docs a word must appear in before it gets scored (the setting's name varies by engine). Always use more than 1; 25 works well on a large forum dataset.
    • 
          
  • The forward index makes the hop possible. The inverted index maps term → docs. To ask "which words appear in these docs?", the engine needs the reverse, doc → terms. That forward index is what search engines already use for facets, and you turn it on per field with doc values. One SKG hop is: query → doc set (inverted index) → terms in those docs (forward index) → score each term.
    • 
          
  • Works best on overlapping text. SKGs need words to repeat across many docs. Forums, tickets, and reviews work well. A wiki with one page per topic gives weak scores.

          An off-the-shelf spellchecker picks the nearby word that appears most in your documents. For example,

§5 · Clicks as Labels

        
Click models and position bias


        

          Users click whatever sits on top, good or not, so raw clicks make biased labels. A click model
          corrects for position before the clicks train anything, and active learning makes sure lower results
          get seen at all.
        


        
    
          
  • Click model spectrum: Cascade vs. SDBN vs. Full DBN. The basic Cascade Model assumes users stop at the first click. SDBN assumes users scan down to the last click and tracks two things per result: how often it gets clicked when seen, and how often that click ends the search. Full DBN adds a "keep scanning" probability γ, since users may give up without a satisfying click, and has to be fitted with EM. SDBN is the special case γ = 1, so it can be computed by simple counting.
    • 
          
  • Position bias and active learning. Users click rank #1 far more often than rank #5 regardless of quality. SDBN corrects for position by counting a result as seen only if it sits at or above the last click. Results below it don't count against it, and skipped results above it count as seen but not clicked. A low-ranked item that still gets clicks is outperforming its position and deserves a boost. Active learning (for example, a Gaussian process with Expected Improvement) explores unseen docs to break self-reinforcing feedback loops.
    • 
        


        

          
SDBN Attractiveness & Beta Prior Smoothing

          
$$P(C_i=1) = P(E_i=1)\cdot P(A_i=1) \qquad\qquad \hat{g} = \frac{\alpha_0 + \text{clicks}}{\alpha_0 + \beta_0 + \text{examines}}$$

          

            E=1 = examined (all ranks ≤ last click)
            Skip = examined but unclicked (negative label)
            W = α₀ + β₀ = prior confidence weight (50–100)

§6 · Machine-Learned Ranking

        
Learning to rank


        

          Hand-tuning boost weights gets brittle once you have dozens of features (BM25 per field, recency, authority,
          personalization). Learning to Rank (LTR) trains a model such as LambdaMART on query-document feature vectors to
          rerank the top 500 lexical candidates.
        


        

          

            
              
LTR ParadigmWhat the Model PredictsTrade-Off

            
            
              
PointwiseSingle relevance score per docSimple, ignores relative order between docs

              
PairwiseWhether doc A beats doc B: w · (x_A − x_B)Strong default; directly models ranking swaps

              
ListwiseWhole list order (training targets NDCG)Best alignment with search metrics (LambdaMART)

            
          

        


        
    
          
  • LTR generalizes; boosting memorizes. Signals boosting stores "for query q, doc d got clicks". A brand-new query has no row, so boosting does nothing. LTR learns patterns instead, like "a strong title match plus a recent date means relevant". Those patterns apply to any query, including one nobody has typed before. That is the "generalized relevance" in the §3 table.
    • 
          
  • Split train and test by query ID (qid). Never split randomly by document row. If the same query lands in both train and test, your model memorizes the query and leaks accuracy.
    • 
        

        

          Go deeper · 3 more notes
          
    
          
  • In-engine feature logging and efi. Extract live BM25 scores and query-time external feature info (efi) directly from Elasticsearch/OpenSearch/Solr (via LTR plugins or named queries) so training features match production scoring without offline re-computation. Hosting the model inside an engine plugin is getting less common. At scale, a request is only as fast as the slowest node (tail latency), so many teams pull the top-N candidates and their features into a separate reranking service instead.
    • 
          
  • What production teams rerank with. Many e-commerce teams rerank with LambdaMART rather than a cross-encoder, because what matters most there is domain features like popularity, price, and PageRank (in job search, commute distance). Embedding similarity goes in as one more feature.
    • 
          
  • SVMRank vs. LambdaMART. SVMRank fits a linear hyperplane that orders each pair of results for a query: the more relevant doc lands on the positive side of w · (x_A − x_B). LambdaMART trains boosted decision trees on gradients weighted by how much each swap would change NDCG, so training targets NDCG even though NDCG itself can't be differentiated.
    • 
          

        


        

          Offline vs. online metrics, and splitting data by query
          

            

              Offline vs. online metric divergence: Offline NDCG gains often miss online A/B
              conversion lifts because of UI layout and presentation bias. Team-draft interleaving mixes results from
              ranker A and ranker B into one list to measure user preference with far higher statistical power than split
              A/B buckets.
            

            

              Query-level data splitting: Partition labeled datasets strictly by
              qid (75% train, 20% test, 5% holdout) so the same query's documents never appear in both train and test. To keep
              paraphrases from leaking too, group near-duplicate queries before splitting.
            

          

        


        
Pairwise Learning to Rank trainer

        

          Before training a linear model, put every feature on the same scale. A
          release_year near 2010 and a title_bm25 between 0 and 8 can't be compared raw: the
          big numbers drown out the small ones, and a linear model like SVMRank finds a bad dividing line. The fix is the
          z-score. Each value becomes "how many standard deviations above or below the average is this?"
        


        

          
Feature Normalization (z-score)

          
$$z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}$$

          

            μ = the feature's average in the training data
            σ = its standard deviation
            Example = mean year 1990, σ = 22 → 2012 becomes +1.0, 1968 becomes −1.0
          

        


        
    
          
  • Which models need it. Scaling matters for some models: linear ones like SVMRank, and neural nets. Tree models like LambdaMART split on raw values (if title_bm25 > 2 …), so scaling doesn't change what they learn.
    • 
          
  • Ship μ and σ with the model. Live queries must be scaled with the training μ and σ, not fresh ones. If serving scales features differently from training, the learned weights silently stop meaning anything.

How LambdaMART works

        

          Many production teams rerank with LambdaMART (see the note above). It works in three
          moves: it starts list-wise, goes pair-wise, and ends up point-wise.
        

        
    
          
  1. Swap and measure (list-wise). For one query, try swapping every pair of results and see how much a list metric like DCG changes. Swapping a great doc up past a bad one might improve DCG by 0.1.
    2. 
          
  2. Turn swaps into "lambdas" (pair-wise). Add up those swap effects for each document. The result, its lambda, says how hard that doc should be pushed up or down. Each pair is weighted by how wrong the current model's order is, so pairs already ordered by a wide margin barely count.
    3. 
          
  3. Fit trees to the lambdas (point-wise). Now each doc has a target number, so it's a normal regression problem. Gradient-boosted decision trees learn to predict the lambdas from features, and the lambdas are recomputed after every tree. Each new small tree fixes the errors of the ones before it, and the final score is the sum of all the trees.
    4. 
        

        
    
          
  • A tree is just if-statements. "If title_bm25 > 2 and overview_bm25 > 2, score 12." Trees capture combinations ("title match and overview agrees") that a single linear weight can't, and they work on raw, unscaled features.
    • 
          
  • Why trees still win in production. The features that matter most are often tabular: popularity, PageRank, distance, price, plus lexical and embedding scores. Trees handle that mix well and are fast to run.
    •

Where the vectors come from

        
    
          
  • One vector per token, not per sentence. The raw output is a list of token vectors. Search needs one vector per query or doc, so they get pooled.
    • 
          
  • Bi-encoder = two-tower model. Same thing, two names. It can be one shared encoder for both sides (like SBERT) or two separate towers. Asymmetric models also mark queries and documents differently (for example "query:" and "passage:" prefixes), because short queries and long documents look different. You can also train your own on search traffic: Reddit trained one on what users actually clicked. Off-the-shelf models only work well on the kind of matching they were trained for, so check before you use one.
    • 
        

        

          Go deeper · 4 more notes
          
    
          
  • Subword tokens. A Transformer like BERT first splits text into WordPiece tokens. Common words stay whole, and rare ones break into pieces, so an unknown error code still gets a vector from its parts. Special tokens mark the start ([CLS]) and the breaks ([SEP]).
    • 
          
  • Attention gives context. Each token looks at every other token in the sentence and blends in what it learns. So reset in "connection reset" ends up with a different vector than reset in "reset my password".
    • 
          
  • Learned by filling in blanks. BERT trains itself with the Cloze test: hide about 15% of the tokens and predict them from the rest (masked language modeling). No labels needed, so it can read huge amounts of text.
    • 
          
  • SBERT: pooling + similarity tuning. Raw BERT vectors are poor at cosine search out of the box. Sentence-BERT averages the token vectors (mean pooling), then fine-tunes the model on sentence-pair data like NLI and STS-B, so that similar meanings really land close together. A name like roberta-base-nli-stsb-mean-tokens spells out the recipe. Mean pooling is the simple way to picture it, but pooling depends on the model, and some weight tokens differently using attention.

What embeddings are good at, and what they can't do

        
    
          
  • They rank; they don't match. Every document gets a similarity score, even a useless one. There's no natural line between "relevant" and "not", and finding a cutoff is a well-known weakness of embedding models. That's why filters and query understanding (§1, §4) still decide what is eligible.
    • 
          
  • Semantic drift. For example, a vector search for "The Hobbit" finds The Hobbit: An Unexpected Journey and other Middle-earth titles, which is great. But it also pulls in Harry Potter, which shares the "wizards and magic" neighborhood. Lexical search fails differently: it matched only the word the once the hobbit pages ran out. Hybrid search (§8) uses each to cover the other.
    • 
          
  • Numbers break embeddings. Models learn a meaning for each token, not a number line. The vector for 2 isn't reliably closer to 3 than to 10, so similarity between numbers is "spiky". Put prices, sizes, and versions in real fields and use range filters or a separate numeric feature.
    • 
          
  • Dimensions aren't human-readable. Toy examples (like Demo 7.3) label each axis with a concept. Real 768-d embeddings spread each concept across many dimensions, which is how a few hundred numbers can hold millions of concepts.
    • 
          
  • Vectors can be added and averaged. Because meaning is position, the midpoint of two vectors lands near things that share both. Example: average a photo of a puppy with a photo of Darth Vader, and the nearest image is a puppy in a Darth Vader costume. Average a sporty car photo with the text "superhero" (in a shared image–text space like CLIP), and you find a superhero on a sporty car.

§8 · Hybrid Search

        
Hybrid search: Reciprocal Rank Fusion vs. Relative Score Fusion

        

          You cannot add raw BM25 scores (unbounded, 0 to ∞) to cosine similarity (bounded,
          -1 to 1). RRF ignores raw scores and sums reciprocal ranks. RSF min-max normalizes
          both lists to [0, 1] and takes a weighted average.
        


        
    
          
  • Reciprocal rank fusion (RRF). Uses only each page's place in each list. Agreement across lists beats a page that tops only one.
    • 
          
  • Relative score fusion (RSF). Rescales each list's scores to 0–1, then takes a weighted average. The catch: the last page in each list always scores 0, however close it was.
    • 
          
  • Match first, rerank second. The lexical match (the AND/OR lookup in the inverted index, §1) decides which pages qualify. BM25 then scores those pages, and a vector score or cross-encoder can reorder them. BM25 is the scoring step after matching, not the matching step itself.
    •

§9 · Fast, Small Vectors

        
Making vector search fast and small


        

          Storing 1 million 1536-dimensional FP32 vectors takes about 6 GB of RAM. Modern vector stacks combine
          Matryoshka Representation Learning (MRL), quantization, and two-stage rescoring to cut memory by 20× to 100×
          with minimal recall loss.
        


        
Finding neighbors fast: HNSW and IVF

        
    
          
  • HNSW (Hierarchical Navigable Small World). A multi-layer proximity graph where upper layers hold long-range skip edges and the bottom layer holds dense local links, routing greedily in O(log N) hops.
    • 
          
  • IVF (Inverted File Index). Partitions vector space into Voronoi cells around k-means centroids (nlist). At query time you scan only the nearest nprobe cells.
    • 
          
  • Quantization plus over-request and rescore. Int8 scalar quantization cuts RAM by 75% with >92% recall; Product Quantization (PQ) compresses even further into sub-vector codebooks. Over-requesting top 50 quantized candidates and rescoring them with full FP32 vectors recovers almost all lost recall; a cross-encoder can then rerank the survivors for relevance. Some newer embedding models ship int8 vectors trained for it, which can be close to lossless compared with FP32.

Smaller vectors: Matryoshka and binary

        
    
          
  • Matryoshka Representation Learning (MRL). Trains embeddings so the most important information sits in the earliest dimensions. If the model was trained with MRL, you can slice a 1536d vector down to its first 256d prefix without retraining. It only works on MRL-trained models, and raw recall falls fast as you cut. In one test, halving the dimensions kept 70% recall, a quarter kept 48%, and an eighth kept 24%. Reranking the candidates with full vectors won most of it back. Most engines also fix the vector size in the schema, so slicing at query time usually isn't possible. In production, MRL is often more of a parlor trick: binary quantization plus reranking usually gives better trade-offs.
    • 
          
  • Binary quantization (1-bit). Converts every float to 0 or 1 (32× smaller than FP32) and scores with hardware XOR + popcount (Hamming distance). The demo below scores a full-precision query against the 1-bit vectors (asymmetric scoring), which engines also offer.
    • 
        


        
The quantization ladder

        

          Every option trades memory for accuracy. One trick makes them safe to use: keep the small,
          quantized vectors in RAM for the fast first pass, keep the full-size vectors on disk, and rerank the
          top candidates with the full vectors. You get close to the original results with far less memory.

          random orthogonal matrix) spreads that outlier across all dimensions. Lengths and dot products don't change,
          because rotation is exact. But the max shrinks, so the same bits describe every value more finely. That's the
          idea behind rotational quantization (TurboQuant).

    3.

    4. 
          
  4. Drag the bits up to 6. With more bits, both get better. Rotation matters most when bits are scarce.

              Keep small quantized vectors in RAM, keep full vectors on disk, and rerank the top hits. That cuts memory
              by 20× to 100× with little recall loss. Next: going beyond one vector per document.

§10 · Beyond One Vector

        
Beyond one vector: ColBERT, SPLADE, and Wormhole Vectors


        

          Compressing a 500-word document into one dense vector loses fine-grained token detail. Two architectures solve
          this from opposite directions without paying full Cross-Encoder latency.
        


        
    
          
  • Late interaction (ColBERT, ColPali). Stores one contextual vector per token at index time. At query time, each query token finds its single highest dot product across all document tokens (MaxSim), then sums those maximums. ColPali applies the same idea to PDF page image patches.
    • 
          
  • Sparse lexical expansion (SPLADE, Doc2Query). SPLADE uses a transformer to predict importance weights across the full vocabulary, expanding short text with synonyms like socket hang up → econnreset, keepalive, tcp so it runs directly inside a fast inverted index. Doc2Query gets a similar effect differently: it generates likely queries and appends them to the document as text.
    • 
        

        

          Go deeper · 3 more notes
          
    
          
  • System One (Decision Models) as rerankers. Compact decision models (such as pplx-decider-flash) score query-document relevance fast enough for production reranking, bridging classic cross-encoders and expensive frontier LLMs. Sizes run from about 1.7B up to roughly 21B–31B for the top benchmark scores, and bigger usually does better. Reranking is only one job. Because they pick from a fixed list of answers, they also handle query classification and query understanding, routing (for example, which team gets this message), and fine-tuned domain classifiers. Decision models can replace a lot of what used to need a full LLM, often fast enough even for regular search. Expect hundreds of ms per rerank call, with big swings on hosted APIs.
    • 
          
  • Why "System One"? The name comes from the two modes of thinking: System 1 is fast and intuitive, System 2 is slow and deliberate. A decision model is built for System 1. It doesn't reason step by step; it picks one answer from a fixed list in a single step. Its confidence comes from the score the model gives each option's token, the same scores an LLM uses to choose its next word. The trade-off: speed and low cost, with a ceiling on accuracy for problems that need real thinking.
    • 
          
  • Watch the word "pairwise". In LTR (§6), pairwise means comparing doc A against doc B. In reranker APIs, "pairwise" often means scoring one (query, doc) pair per call, and "listwise" means sending all candidates together.

§11 · Evaluation

        
Measuring relevance: NDCG, recall, and MRR


        

          Without offline metrics on a judgment list, tuning search is guesswork. NDCG@k rewards putting graded relevant
          docs (0 to 3) at the top, discounts lower ranks by log₂(i + 1), and
          divides by the ideal ordering so scores stay between 0 and 1.
        


        
    
          
  • G (Gain). Points for one result from its grade: 2^rel − 1. Grade 3 earns 7; grade 0 earns 0.
    • 
          
  • C (Cumulative). Add up the points of every result in the top k.
    • 
          
  • D (Discounted). Shrink each result by its rank: rank 1 counts 100%, rank 2 63%, rank 3 50%, rank 4 43%, rank 5 39%, and rank 10 only 29%.
    • 
          
  • N (Normalized). Divide by the score of the ideal order (IDCG) so queries stay comparable on [0, 1].
    • 
          
  • @k (Cutoff). Only the top k results count. Anything past rank k adds nothing.
    • 
        


        

          
Discounted Cumulative Gain & Normalization

          
$$\text{DCG}_k = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \frac{2^{\text{rel}_i} - 1}{\log_2(i + 1)} \qquad\qquad \text{NDCG}_k = \frac{\text{DCG}_k}{\text{IDCG}_k} \in [0, 1]$$

  • Pair NDCG@10 with recall@50 or @100. Recall tells you whether the right docs entered the candidate pool; NDCG@10 tells you whether the final order is good.
    • 
          
  • For agents and RAG, order matters less. When an LLM reads the top 10–20 results, it matters much less whether the best one is #1 or #8 (though LLMs still favor some positions in their input). Set-level recall (Recall@20) often tracks answer quality better than NDCG@10. NDCG still matters for ranked lists that people scan.
    • 
          
  • Graded vs. pass/fail labels. Simple good/not-good labels are faster to collect and more consistent between raters, and often enough for agent evals. Graded labels (0–3) still matter when you train a ranker like LTR, because it has to learn why one good result beats another.
    • 
          
  • MRR for one-right-answer search. When each question has one right answer, or labels mark about one relevant passage per query (as in MS MARCO, which reports MRR@10), score each query as 1 / rank of the first right answer and average over queries: rank 1 → 1.0, rank 2 → 0.5, rank 4 → 0.25. That's Mean Reciprocal Rank. RAG is different: it wants several useful passages, so recall-style metrics fit it better.
    • 
          
  • Unjudged docs count as irrelevant. So a new system that surfaces good but never-labeled pages gets penalized unfairly. Check how much of your top 10 is labeled, and check whether your library uses linear gain (rel) or exponential gain (2^rel − 1) before comparing numbers.
    • 
        

        

          Go deeper · 5 more notes
          
    
          
  • The NDCG paradox. NDCG divides by the best order of the docs you judged. If every judged doc is only marginally relevant (say all grade 1), the best order is just as mediocre, so any order of them scores NDCG = 1.0 even though nothing is good. (If all are grade 0, NDCG is undefined; most libraries report 0.) Some teams divide by the best possible DCG (all top grades) instead.
    • 
          
  • IDCG uses all judged docs for the query. A relevant document you never retrieved still lowers your score.
    • 
          
  • Calibrate LLM-as-judge labels. Check agreement against a human-labeled sample (e.g., Krippendorff's alpha) before trusting NDCG deltas. Don't assume humans are the gold standard. Paid human raters are often noisy and rushed. LLM labels are consistent (though they can all share the same bias), and a judging rubric can be fixed and re-run like code. In published comparisons, LLM labels have matched real searchers' preferences better than paid crowd labels.
    • 
          
  • Expected Reciprocal Rank (ERR) and Session-Based Eval. ERR models a cascade user who scans down the list and stops at each result with a probability that grows with its grade. Session-based evaluation scores the whole multi-query journey rather than isolated queries.
    • 
          
  • BlitzRank tournaments and synthetic pairwise judgments. BlitzRank asks an LLM to rank small groups of documents, records every "A beat B" as an edge in a graph, and fills in pairs that never met by assuming wins carry over (A > B and B > C means A > C). LLMs aren't always consistent, so you get loops like A > B > C > A. BlitzRank treats each loop as a tie and groups those docs together (the paper calls them strongly connected components). It only sorts the top M you ask for. Below that the order isn't set, so don't read it as a ranking. The same pairwise pattern can bootstrap cold-start LTR datasets from clustered passages.

Extractive QA, RAG grounding, and multimodal search

        

          Modern search feeds answers directly to users or language models. In every
          case, generation quality is capped by retrieval recall: if the right passage never enters the context window,
          the model cannot cite it.
        


        
    
          
  • Extractive QA: retriever-reader. The search engine retrieves candidate passages (maximizing Recall@k). A fine-tuned reader model predicts two token distributions, P_start(i) and P_end(j), ranks spans by P_start(i) × P_end(j), and abstains when the [CLS] null score (P_start(CLS) × P_end(CLS)) beats the best span.
    • 
          
  • Grounded RAG and abstention. The LLM is constrained to answer strictly from retrieved passages with inline citations. If no chunk clears your similarity threshold τ, skip generation and return "not found" rather than hallucinating. A raw embedding cutoff is fragile. Embeddings rank; they don't match, and picking a cutoff is a well-known weakness of embedding models. Model scores also aren't true probabilities for your data. Use query understanding and filters to decide what's eligible, put thresholds on a reranker's score instead, or have an LLM check citations before answering.
    • 
        

        

          Go deeper · 5 more notes
          
    
          
  • Multimodal retrieval. Models like CLIP project text and images into one shared embedding space, letting a text query retrieve diagrams, screenshots, or products using the same ANN index.
    • 
          
  • Golden sets and QA error taxonomy. Bootstrap a machine-labeled silver set, then audit with humans using four classes: 1 Correct, 0 Fixed, -1 Impossible/Abstain, and -2 Incorrect (a known-wrong span kept as a negative example).
    • 
          
  • Token offset alignment and SQuAD EM vs. F1. Long passages are sliced with sliding doc_stride windows while mapping character offsets to subword tokens. Extractive readers are scored with Exact Match (EM: exact equality after normalizing case, punctuation, and articles) and token-level F1 (partial overlap).
    • 
          
  • HyDE (Hypothetical Document Embeddings). For tough questions, ask an LLM to draft a hypothetical answer first and embed that draft so vector search matches document phrasing instead of short question phrasing.
    • 
          
  • Programmatic prompt optimization with DSPy. Instead of hand-tweaking fragile prompt strings, compile signatures and few-shot examples against an objective metric (Exact Match, F1, schema validity).

Semantic caching and optimizing search for agents

        

          Classic search was built for humans scanning 10 blue links in one shot. AI agents search differently: they
          decompose questions, run multiple tool calls, inspect gaps, and reformulate queries before answering.
        


        

          

            
              
DimensionSearch for HumansSearch for Agents

            
            
              
Latency budgetStrict (about 100–200 ms, single pass)Looser overall (seconds to minutes across many calls), but each search call must still be fast because it runs many times

Query styleShort, ambiguous 2–3 word queriesIterative, self-rewriting, tool-filtered queries

            
          

        


        

          Go deeper · 1 more note
          
    
          
  • Video RAG: MomentSearch architecture. Video retrieval indexes two things: overlapping transcript windows and video frames. Frames must be heavily thinned and deduplicated first, because 24–60 nearly identical frames per second flood the index. A query runs three searches at once (BM25 on transcripts, vectors on transcripts, CLIP on frames). The two transcript lists are merged, and only then are speech and frames grouped into timestamped "moments" for reranking, an LLM check, and a timestamped answer. Plain transcripts say what was said and when, but not who. A multimodal LLM can watch the video and mark speaker changes.
